1 · The process

Removing sulfur from hot metal before making steel

Sulfur makes steel brittle: it reduces ductility and impact toughness. To produce low-sulfur steels, the hot metal coming from the blast furnace is treated in the ladle before going to the oxygen converter.

In powder injection, a refractory lance immersed in the bath blows an inert gas (argon or nitrogen) carrying the powdered reagent. The particles rise with the bubbles, react with the dissolved sulfur, and the sulfide formed floats up to the slag, where it is trapped.

Three reagents are used, alone or mixed. Lime is the cheapest; calcium carbide and magnesium desulfurize faster but cost more. The paper studies each one alone (mono-injection) and in mixtures (co-injection).

Desulfurization reactions
  • CaO + [S] → CaS + (O)lime
  • CaC₂ + [S] → CaS + 2Ccalcium carbide
  • Mg + [S] → MgSmagnesium

[S] is the sulfur dissolved in the hot metal. The solid product rises to the slag.

2 · The model

From the continuity equation to a single exponential

The paper starts from the general sulfur balance in the ladle and, with two physical assumptions, reduces it to an ordinary differential equation with a closed-form solution.

Result · eq. 6S(t) = Se + (S0 − Se) e−k t

Sulfur decays exponentially from the initial value S0 toward the equilibrium value Se; the whole problem comes down to knowing the rate constant k.

Show the step-by-step derivation (eqs. 1 to 6)
  1. Sulfur balance · eq. 1∂S/∂t + v·∇S = DS ∇²S + rS

    Continuity equation: sulfur accumulates, travels with the flow (convection), diffuses and disappears by reaction at rate rS. The paper writes it in cylindrical coordinates; here it is in compact form.

  2. Axial transport · eq. 2∂S/∂t + vz ∂S/∂z = DS ∂²S/∂z² + rS

    Bubbles move mostly upward, so only the vertical direction z is kept.

  3. Well-stirred tank · eq. 3dS/dt = rS

    Diffusion is small compared with convection and gas stirring leaves small concentration gradients: sulfur only changes with time.

  4. First-order kinetics · eqs. 4 and 5rS = −k (S − Se)    k = k0 e−Ea/RT

    The desulfurization rate is proportional to the distance from equilibrium Se. The constant k depends on temperature through Arrhenius; the model is isothermal, so k is fixed during injection.

  5. Solution · eq. 6S(t) = Se + (S0 − Se) e−k t

    Sulfur decays exponentially from S0 toward Se. Everything comes down to knowing k.

Where does k come from?

For lime, k is computed from the physics of injection, in three chained steps.

  1. Bubble rise · eq. 9vb = g (ρM − ρg) db218 μM

    Stokes' law: the bubble rises faster the larger the density difference and its diameter, and slower the more viscous the metal.

  2. Residence time · eq. 8tR = hivb

    The particle rides with the bubble from the lance tip to the surface. A deeper lance gives it more contact time with the metal.

  3. Lime rate constant · eq. 7, after OeterskCaO = 6 β tR qCaO ρMdp wM ρCaO

    It grows with the mass transfer coefficient β, the residence time and the lime feed rate. The term 6/dp is the area per unit volume of a sphere: finer particles offer more surface and react faster. A ladle with more metal dilutes the effect.

Mixtures and half-life

  1. Co-injection · eq. 15km = Σ xi ki

    The rate constant of a mixture is the average of those of each powder, weighted by its mole fraction xi. The compositions in table 3 are in weight percent and are converted to mole fractions with each powder's molar mass.

  2. Half-life · eq. 16th = ln 2k

    Time for the sulfur still to be removed, S − Se, to halve. It does not depend on the initial sulfur, so it is a fair way to compare reagents.

The paper's conclusions

  1. Carrier gas bubbles stir the bath; the CFD simulation supports the well-stirred tank assumption.
  2. The lime mono-injection model, validated against literature data, represents the process quantitatively.
  3. A mixture rate constant based on mole fraction and each powder's rate constant is proposed.
  4. Lime content governs co-injection speed, especially in the first half: more lime, longer half-life.
  5. To speed up desulfurization, lime in the mix should be reduced; since it is the cheapest powder, the decision requires an economic, technical and productivity analysis at plant level.

5 · 3D CFD simulation

Inside the bath: explore the 3D simulation

The paper's CFD

To check the well-stirred tank assumption, the paper simulates the ladle in 2D with Fluent 6.1: transient, isothermal flow, the VOF model for the two phases and standard k-ε turbulence. Gas leaves two nozzles at 45° at the model's bubble velocity, with k = 0.01 vb² at the inlet and ε computed from the nozzle diameter. At 5 s the bubbles rise next to the lance and many spread toward the walls in the upper part (figure 3), and the streamlines show stirring down to the bottom of the ladle (figure 4).

This twin's CFD

Here the same ladle was simulated in full 3D with OpenFOAM. With bubbles just over 1 mm in a bath more than 2.5 m deep, the gas is treated as a continuous phase coexisting with the metal (Euler–Euler model) instead of tracking every interface as in VOF; turbulence is mixture k-ε. The lance depth, the two 20 mm nozzles at 45°, the exit velocity and the paper's inlet k and ε are kept.

Each case simulates 60 s of flow and the fields are averaged between 20 s and 60 s, once circulation is established. Argon rises next to the lance and, at the surface, spreads toward the walls; the metal sinks along the sides and forms a large recirculation loop on each side of the lance reaching the bottom: the same picture as figures 3 and 4.

Simulation assumptions

From the paper: 150 t mass, lance depth, two 20 mm nozzles at 45°, bubble diameter, hot metal density and viscosity, inlet k and ε. Assumed here as typical of a 150 t ladle: 3.2 m diameter, 0.2 m square lance, 0.4 m freeboard, 1.7 N/m surface tension and an isothermal process at 1350 °C.

6 · How it was made

From the paper to the twin

  1. Reproduce the model. Equations 6 to 9, 15 and 16 were programmed in Python with the values of tables 1 to 3 and checked against the published results: bubble velocity, residence time, lime rate constant, final sulfur and the constants of the five mixtures.
  2. Bring it to the browser. The same model runs in JavaScript inside this page; it was compared against the Python version at 28 points, with no differences.
  3. Simulate the flow. Twelve 3D runs in OpenFOAM: a mesh study (coarse, medium and fine), a sweep of 3 gas flows × 3 lance depths and the base case on the fine mesh with the nozzles modeled. They ran on a personal computer, with automatic pause and resume.
  4. Extract the fields. ParaView and Python take from each run the vertical cut through the lance, the average flow and the streamlines.
  5. Visualize. Blender (Cycles) builds the ladle cut from those fields; three.js shows the flow in 3D on this page.

Scope

  • As in the paper, the process is isothermal; eq. 5 is not used because it needs k0 and Ea.
  • The slag is not modeled.
  • The CFD resolves the gas and metal flow, not the chemistry: the sulfur curves come from the published model.
  • The ladle diameter, the lance cross-section and some properties are not given in the paper; typical values for a 150 t ladle were used (see the assumptions in section 5).
